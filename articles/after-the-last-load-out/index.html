What remains after the last load-out

Arriving from outside in a small town, hiring close by and building trust before you need it: notes from a port on Denmark’s west coast.

By Daniel Zonneveld·Thyborøn, Denmark·October 2026·3 min read

Can someone who comes from outside, for a job with an end date, become part of a place? In Thyborøn the question was not theoretical: the town is small, everyone knows everyone, and we came from another culture, another language, another context. Over time it became clear that the answer does not depend on where you come from, but on how you arrive.

A project settles into a community the way an organ settles into a body. If it works in isolation, like a foreign body, it receives and returns only what is in the contract; if it connects, it enters the circulation, helps and is helped. That is why, from months before the first load-out, we set out to get to know the suppliers in the region, how they lived and what mattered to them, and we spent time with them outside meetings. We often chose to hire locally, even when bringing everything in from the nominally cheapest source seemed the obvious choice. It did not always cost more: the mobile toolbox-talk rooms, for example, came in cheaper than we had expected.

The proof came at a concrete moment. The External Working Platforms we were about to receive required specific preparation of the area, because of the transport from the quay and the storage itself, and there was little time left before the first load-out. We could go ahead without it and accept a slower, riskier operation, or bring in a renowned company from a major hub such as Copenhagen or Esbjerg. We chose to call a local supplier we knew well, who had already helped us with much smaller jobs. He came on a Friday after hours, understood the challenge and, after a few phone calls, arranged with his team and his subcontractors for the earthworks to start on Monday morning. Before the end of Wednesday, the area was ready for transport between the quay and the storage area. When the vessel arrived, there was no delay at all on that account.

Could we have asked this of someone who knew us only by a purchase order number? Whoever turns up on a Friday after hours does not do it for a contract, but for a relationship. The farmer does not dig the well on the day of the drought, and trust is not built on the day you need it either. I am not saying that price and schedule do not matter; I am saying that, that week, it was the relationship that secured the schedule. Good relationships were not an add-on to the planning; they were part of it.

The cost that does not appear in the budget is saudade, the longing for what was, and that is why many treat each job as “just another project”; I never could. A house can be taken down and its occupant carries on: the project was the house, the friendships and the collaboration are the occupant, and the longing itself proves that they remained, because no one misses what never existed. For those who spend their lives moving from project to project, perhaps this is the question to carry into the next one: what will remain when the last load-out has left?

Daniel Zonneveld is the founder of Zonneveld Engineering. He writes from the offshore projects he has worked on.
